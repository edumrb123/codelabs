# Prompt: A 3Blue1Brown-Style Video on the History of Art

Copy everything below the line into your AI tool of choice (Claude, ChatGPT, etc.).
It asks for a full narration script, a scene-by-scene visual plan, and Manim code.

---

## ROLE

You are a writer and animator working in the style of Grant Sanderson's
**3Blue1Brown**, paired with a careful art historian. Your job is to produce a
~30-minute explainer video, **"How Humans Learned to Make Pictures: A Visual
History of Art"**, covering art from the earliest known marks to the present.

## WHAT "3BLUE1BROWN STYLE" MEANS HERE

- **Start with a question, not a timeline.** Open with a puzzle the viewer
  feels, e.g.: *"A flat surface has two dimensions. The world has three. Every
  picture ever made is a solution to that mismatch, and to the question of what
  a picture is for. How did humans keep changing their answer?"*
- **One unifying thread** runs through the whole video, so it reads as a story
  instead of a list. Use these three recurring "axes", drawn on screen as a
  simple 3-axis diagram that comes back at each era:
  1. **Space:** how is depth shown? (hierarchical scale → overlapping →
     linear perspective → fragmented/multiple viewpoints → no illusion at all)
  2. **Purpose:** who is the art for? (ritual/magic → gods and kings → church →
     merchants and collectors → the artist → the market/the public/the idea)
  3. **Medium/technology:** what tools made new art possible? (pigment and
     stone → bronze casting → tempera/fresco → oil paint → printmaking →
     photography → industrial materials → video, digital, AI)
- **Visual intuition before vocabulary.** Show the idea, then name it
  ("this is called *contrapposto*").
- **Minimal, elegant visuals:** dark background (#1C1C1C), a restrained palette
  (blue #58C4DD, yellow #FFFF00, a warm red, plus white), smooth transforms,
  shapes that morph into one another, lines that draw themselves on.
- **Calm, curious, first-person-plural narration** ("Let's look at...",
  "Notice what happens if..."). Ask the viewer to pause and guess sometimes.
- **Mathematical moments where they naturally fit,** animated properly:
  - Linear perspective: orthogonals converging to a vanishing point, the
    horizon line, a tiled floor (Alberti's construction) built step by step,
    then laid over Masaccio's *Holy Trinity*.
  - Contrapposto as a tilt of the hip and shoulder axes in opposite directions.
  - Color: optical mixing vs. pigment mixing (Seurat), complementary colors on
    a color wheel (Impressionists, Van Gogh).
  - Cubism: one object projected from several viewpoints, then the
    projections combined on one plane.
  - Abstraction as removing information step by step (e.g. Mondrian's
    tree series, c. 1908–1912).
- **End with a callback** to the opening question and a short reflective
  closing line. No sponsor segment, and no Pi creatures (those are 3b1b's own
  characters).

## ACCURACY RULES (very important)

1. Use **dates that reflect current scholarly consensus** and mark them with
   "c." when approximate. For prehistory, say "years ago" and give ranges.
2. When a date or attribution is **contested, say so briefly on screen**
   instead of picking one silently (for example, the authorship of Duchamp's
   *Fountain*, the dates of the earliest cave art, Neanderthal art claims).
3. **Do not repeat popular myths.** In particular:
   - Classical Greek and Roman statues were **painted** (polychromy), not
     pure white.
   - There is **no good evidence** the Parthenon or the *Mona Lisa* were
     designed around the golden ratio. If you mention it, debunk it.
   - Michelangelo painted the Sistine ceiling **standing on scaffolding,
     head tilted back**, not lying down.
   - The "Dark Ages" were not an artistic void.
   - "Impressionism" was named after Monet's *Impression, Sunrise* (1872)
     by a mocking critic (Louis Leroy) reviewing the 1874 exhibition.
4. **Not only Europe.** The Western canon can be the spine, but show that
   art happened everywhere, at the same time, with its own answers to the
   same questions (see the "parallel" items below). Avoid calling
   non-European traditions "primitive."
5. Say "earliest known" rather than "first": new discoveries keep pushing
   dates back.
6. If you are unsure of a fact, **flag it with [VERIFY]** in the script
   instead of inventing it.
7. **Image rights:** many 20th/21st-century works are still under copyright.
   In the visual plan, use either public-domain images (museum open-access
   collections such as the Met, Rijksmuseum, Art Institute of Chicago,
   National Gallery of Art) or **simplified vector reconstructions** that show
   composition and structure, labelled with title, artist, date and collection.

## STRUCTURE (chapters, rough timings)

Use the works below as the backbone. You may add or swap a few, but keep the
key pieces and keep the dates accurate.

**0. Cold open (1 min).** The 2D/3D problem and the "what is a picture for?"
question. Introduce the 3-axis diagram.

**1. Prehistory: the first marks (3 min)**
- Engraved ochre, Blombos Cave, South Africa (c. 75,000–100,000 years ago):
  abstract pattern before figures.
- Lion-Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel (c. 40,000 years ago): imagination, a
  creature that doesn't exist.
- Figurative cave painting in Sulawesi, Indonesia (dated to at least ~45,000–
  51,000 years ago in recent studies; [VERIFY latest dating]).
- Chauvet (c. 36,000 years ago) and Lascaux (c. 17,000 years ago): using the
  rock's bumps for volume, overlapping animals suggesting movement.
- Venus of Willendorf (c. 30,000–25,000 years ago).
- Göbekli Tepe (c. 9500 BCE): monumental carving before farming cities.
- Purpose axis: we don't really know. Say so honestly and list hypotheses.

**2. The first civilizations: art as order and power (3 min)**
- Narmer Palette (c. 3100 BCE); Egyptian "composite view" (head and legs in
  profile, eye and shoulders frontal) animated as a diagram; hierarchical
  scale; the canon of proportions grid.
- Standard of Ur (c. 2600 BCE); Stele of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE).
- Bust of Nefertiti (c. 1345 BCE): the brief naturalism of the Amarna period.
- Parallels: Olmec colossal heads (c. 1200–900 BCE); Shang dynasty bronzes
  (c. 1600–1046 BCE); Nok terracottas, Nigeria (first millennium BCE).

**3. Greece and Rome: the body in motion (3.5 min)**
- Kouros (stiff, Egyptian-like, c. 600 BCE) → Kritios Boy (c. 480 BCE) →
  Polykleitos' Doryphoros (c. 440 BCE, known from Roman copies): animate the
  arrival of contrapposto.
- Parthenon (447–432 BCE): proportion, and the slight curves (entasis) that
  correct optical effects.
- Hellenistic drama: Laocoön (dating debated, c. 1st century BCE–1st century CE).
- Rome: realistic portrait busts, Augustus of Prima Porta, Pantheon dome
  (c. 113–125 CE), Pompeii frescoes with early, inconsistent perspective.
- Show polychromy reconstructions.
- Parallel: Terracotta Army (c. 210 BCE); early Buddhist art and Gandhara
  sculpture blending Greek and Indian styles.

**4. Faith and the flattened world: c. 300–1300 (3 min)**
- Why naturalism *recedes*: not lost skill but a new goal, a window onto the
  spiritual, not the physical. Space axis goes "backward" on purpose.
- Byzantine mosaics and icons (Hagia Sophia, 532–537; San Vitale, Ravenna,
  c. 547); gold background as heavenly non-space.
- Book of Kells (c. 800); Bayeux Embroidery ("Tapestry", 1070s).
- Gothic: Chartres Cathedral (largely 1194–c. 1220), light and stained glass.
- Parallels: Islamic geometric art and the Alhambra (mainly 13th–14th c.),
  animated tessellations; Song dynasty landscape painting, e.g. Fan Kuan's
  *Travelers among Mountains and Streams* (c. 1000) with its shifting
  perspective; Ajanta caves (c. 2nd c. BCE – 5th c. CE); Chola bronzes (Shiva
  Nataraja, c. 10th–11th c.); Ife heads (c. 12th–15th c.).

**5. The Renaissance: the window (4 min). The math centerpiece.**
- Giotto, Arena (Scrovegni) Chapel (c. 1305): weight, emotion, near-3D space.
- Brunelleschi's perspective demonstration (c. 1415–1420); Alberti's *De
  pictura* (1435). Build linear perspective from scratch on screen.
- Masaccio, *Holy Trinity* (c. 1427): overlay the vanishing point.
- Northern Renaissance and oil paint: Van Eyck, *Ghent Altarpiece* (1432) and
  *Arnolfini Portrait* (1434); the convex mirror.
- Botticelli, *Birth of Venus* (c. 1485); Leonardo, *Last Supper* (c. 1495–98,
  vanishing point at Christ's head) and *Mona Lisa* (c. 1503–1519, sfumato,
  atmospheric perspective); Michelangelo, *David* (1501–04) and Sistine
  ceiling (1508–12); Raphael, *School of Athens* (1509–11).
- Dürer: printmaking spreads images; his perspective machine woodcut (1525).
- Purpose axis: wealthy merchant patrons (the Medici) and humanism.

**6. Baroque to Rococo: drama, light, the viewer (2.5 min)**
- Mannerism briefly (Parmigianino, *Madonna with the Long Neck*, c. 1535–40).
- Caravaggio, *The Calling of Saint Matthew* (1599–1600): chiaroscuro and
  tenebrism, light as a spotlight.
- Bernini, *Ecstasy of Saint Teresa* (1647–52); Counter-Reformation purpose.
- Dutch Golden Age, an open art market: Rembrandt, *The Night Watch* (1642);
  Vermeer, *Girl with a Pearl Earring* (c. 1665) and the camera obscura
  debate (flag as debated).
- Velázquez, *Las Meninas* (1656): who is looking at whom? Animate the
  sightlines.
- Rococo: Fragonard, *The Swing* (c. 1767).

**7. Revolutions: 1780–1860 (2.5 min)**
- Neoclassicism: David, *Oath of the Horatii* (1784).
- Romanticism: Goya, *The Third of May 1808* (1814); Friedrich, *Wanderer
  above the Sea of Fog* (c. 1818); Géricault, *Raft of the Medusa* (1818–19);
  Delacroix, *Liberty Leading the People* (1830).
- Realism: Courbet, *The Stone Breakers* (1849, destroyed 1945).
- **Turning point: photography (Daguerre's process announced 1839).** On the
  Medium axis, painting no longer needs to be the best recorder of reality.
  So what is it for now?

**8. Modernism I: breaking the window (3 min)**
- Manet, *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* and *Olympia* (both 1863): flatness,
  provocation.
- Impressionism: Monet, *Impression, Sunrise* (1872); paint in tubes allows
  work outdoors; light and time as the subject (Rouen Cathedral series,
  1890s).
- Japanese woodblock influence: Hokusai, *The Great Wave off Kanagawa*
  (c. 1831).
- Post-Impressionism: Seurat, *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte* (1884–86, optical
  mixing); Van Gogh, *The Starry Night* (1889); Cézanne's Mont Sainte-Victoire
  (treat nature by "the cylinder, the sphere, the cone").
- Munch, *The Scream* (1893); Klimt, *The Kiss* (1907–08).

**9. Modernism II: the picture becomes the idea (3 min)**
- Picasso, *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907), and the influence of African
  and Iberian art (handle with nuance); Cubism with Braque. Animate multiple
  viewpoints.
- Futurism briefly (Boccioni, *Unique Forms of Continuity in Space*, 1913).
- Abstraction: Kandinsky (c. 1910–1913, priority debated, mention Hilma af
  Klint's earlier abstract works from 1906); Malevich, *Black Square* (1915);
  Mondrian's grid compositions (1920s).
- Duchamp, *Fountain* (1917): art as a choice, not a craft (note the
  contested attribution to Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven).
- Surrealism: Dalí, *The Persistence of Memory* (1931); Magritte, *The
  Treachery of Images* (1929), "this is not a pipe" as a lesson about
  representation itself.
- Picasso, *Guernica* (1937); Frida Kahlo's self-portraits.

**10. After 1945: art about art (2.5 min)**
- Abstract Expressionism: Pollock's drip paintings (1947–50); Rothko's color
  fields. Center of the art world moves to New York.
- Pop Art: Warhol, *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962); Lichtenstein (Ben-Day dots,
  a nice callback to Seurat's dots).
- Minimalism and Conceptual art (Donald Judd; Sol LeWitt, instructions as the
  artwork). The Space axis reaches zero illusion: the object is just itself.
- Performance, land and installation art (Marina Abramović; Robert Smithson,
  *Spiral Jetty*, 1970); Yayoi Kusama's Infinity Rooms.
- Basquiat (early 1980s): street culture enters the museum.

**11. Now: 1990s to today (2 min)**
- Globalization of the art world; video and digital art; photography as fine
  art.
- Banksy's self-shredding *Girl with Balloon* (2018, retitled *Love Is in the
  Bin*): the market as medium.
- NFTs: Beeple's *Everydays* sold for ~$69 million (2021).
- AI-generated images (e.g. the 2022 Colorado State Fair controversy): the
  newest point on the Medium axis, and the same old question of what a
  picture is for. Stay neutral and present the debate.

**12. Closing (1 min).** Replay all three axes as one animated path through
history. Return to the opening question: maybe the history of art is less a
march of progress and more a record of the different answers humans gave to
"what should a picture do?" End on a quiet image: a handprint stencil from a
cave wall morphing into a modern screen.
